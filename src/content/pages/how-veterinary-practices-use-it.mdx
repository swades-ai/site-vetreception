## The situation, in the words practices use

The calls that hurt are not the routine ones. They are the ones that arrive while the veterinarian is in surgery, while the front desk is checking in a euthanasia, or at 3am when nobody is in the building. A practice running one to six veterinarians with a PIMS and no overnight front desk has no third option: the phone rings out, the caller dials the next clinic, and the record of that call is a voicemail nobody has time to work through the next morning.

Owners describe this in the same handful of ways. "We miss calls during surgery." "After hours goes to a machine." "The voicemail is a mess by Monday." None of those are staffing failures. They are the predictable result of a phone line that requires a human to be free at the exact moment a client calls.

## What happens today

Most practices patch this with one of three things, and each has a specific failure mode.

**Voicemail.** The caller hears a recording, leaves a name and a number, and hangs up. If the pet is vomiting or the dog is limping, the owner has no idea whether to wait until morning or drive to the emergency hospital forty minutes away. Many do not leave a message at all.

**An answering service.** A human in a call centre picks up. They can take a message and read a script. They cannot see your schedule, they cannot write into your PIMS, and they are working from a generic urgency model rather than your protocol. The message arrives as a voicemail or an email that the front desk still has to convert into an appointment.

**The front desk absorbing it.** Someone answers between clients, puts the caller on hold, and comes back. This works until the waiting room fills, and then it stops working without warning.

## What happens with VetReception

VetReception answers every call a veterinary practice cannot get to — after hours, during surgery, and at three in the morning — triages it against the practice's own written emergency protocol rather than a generic script, books the appointment straight into the PIMS, and leaves the front desk a written summary instead of a voicemail to work through.

The triage step is the part that differs from an answering service. Before go-live, the practice supplies its own written emergency protocol — the same document the veterinarians already use to decide what is seen tonight and what is seen tomorrow. The call is matched against that document. A practice that wants every hit-by-car seen immediately gets that. A practice that wants a limping dog booked into the next available slot gets that instead. [Emergency vet call triage](/blog/emergency-vet-call-triage/) walks through how the matching works on a live call.

Booking is written into the PIMS, not into a separate calendar. The appointment appears where the rest of the day's appointments appear, with the client and patient attached. See [VetReception integrations](/works-with-the-software-veterinary-practices-already-run/) for the practice management systems this covers, including [Avimark integration](/blog/avimark-integration/) and [Shepherd veterinary software integration](/blog/shepherd-veterinary-software-integration/).

The AI never gives clinical advice. Anything clinical — a dosage question, a wound that needs eyes on it, a pet that has eaten something it should not have — is routed to a named person at the practice rather than answered. The system's job is to answer, to sort by the practice's own rules, and to book. It is not to practise.

Every call ends with a written summary: who called, what the pet was doing, which protocol line applied, and what was booked. That summary goes to the front desk. It is not a voicemail to be listened to and transcribed.

## What it does not do

It does not replace the front desk. The desk still checks clients in, takes payment, handles the walk-ins, and makes the judgement calls that need a person standing in the building. What changes is that the desk stops being the phone queue.

It does not make clinical decisions. The protocol is the practice's, written by the practice, and the system applies it. If the protocol changes, the behaviour changes.

It does not work without a PIMS. Booking is written into the PIMS, so a practice running entirely on paper is not a fit.

## What to have ready before go-live

Three things, and the first is the one that matters.

1. The written emergency protocol, in whatever form it already exists — a Word document, a laminated sheet at the desk, a page in the practice manual.
2. Access details for the PIMS, so appointments can be written in.
3. The list of named people clinical calls route to, with the hours each is reachable.

Setup is a configuration exercise, not a build. The practice is not asked to invent a triage model; it is asked to hand over the one it already uses.
