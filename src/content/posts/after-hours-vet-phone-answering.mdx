After hours vet phone answering covers the calls nobody at the practice is free to pick up: overnight, through surgery blocks, and at three in the morning. The requirement is not a message service — it is a call that gets answered, triaged against the practice's own written emergency protocol, and booked into the PIMS before the caller hangs up.

## What has to happen on the call

A caller at 2am with a vomiting dog is not asking for a callback in the morning. They are asking whether to come in now, wait until opening, or drive to the emergency hospital across town. That decision belongs to the practice, not to the answering service, and it is written down somewhere in the practice — a binder, a shared drive, the onboarding pack a locum is handed on their first shift.

VetReception triages against that document. The practice supplies its protocol; the system asks the questions the protocol asks and routes the call the way the protocol routes it. A generic urgency model that ranks every call on a five-point scale produces the wrong answer for a practice that treats a blocked cat as an immediate come-in and a limping dog as a next-morning appointment.

Three things have to be true on every call:

- The call is answered, not queued, including the ones that arrive at 3am and the ones that arrive while the practice is in surgery.
- The triage outcome matches the practice's protocol, not a script written for a different clinic.
- The appointment is written into the PIMS, so the morning front desk sees a booked slot rather than a note to call someone back.

After the call, the front desk gets a written summary of what was said and what was decided. That is the difference between a receptionist starting the day with a list of appointments and a receptionist starting the day with eleven voicemails to work through before the first client arrives.

## Why a voicemail is the expensive option

The cost of an unanswered after-hours call is not the call. It is the client who rang a competitor, the emergency that arrived four hours later than it should have, and the receptionist time spent reconstructing a conversation from a forty-second recording.

A practice running one overnight line and no front desk has three realistic options: let it ring to voicemail, pay an answering service to take a message, or route it to a service that can triage and book. The first two both end with a human at the practice calling back. The third ends with an appointment on the schedule.

## How triage against your own protocol works

Protocols differ between practices more than vendors admit. Two small-animal practices twenty minutes apart will disagree about whether a cat that has not urinated for twelve hours is an immediate or a first-thing call. A triage system that cannot hold both answers is not usable by both practices.

Setup runs through the practice's written protocol, question by question, and the routing that follows each answer. Where the protocol is silent, the call routes to a named person rather than to a default. The AI never gives clinical advice; anything clinical goes to the person the practice nominated, at the number the practice nominated.

That boundary matters for two reasons. It keeps the practice inside its own clinical governance, and it means the system is not making a judgement it has no standing to make. The system's job is to ask, route, and book.

## Booking into the PIMS, not a separate calendar

A booking that lives in a separate calendar is a booking someone has to re-enter. The value of writing the appointment into the PIMS is that the schedule the morning team opens is the schedule that already contains the overnight calls.

## What the front desk receives in the morning

Every call produces a written summary: who called, what the protocol question set established, what was booked, and what was routed to a person. It is a record, not a recording.

The practical effect is that the first hour of the day is spent on clients rather than on reconstruction. A receptionist who would otherwise listen to nine voicemails and call nine people back instead opens a schedule with the overnight bookings already in it and a short list of calls that need a human follow-up.

## Comparison: voicemail, answering service, and protocol triage

| | Voicemail | Message-taking answering service | Protocol triage with PIMS booking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answered at 3am | No | Yes | Yes |
| Triage | None | Generic script | The practice's own written emergency protocol |
| Booking | None | None | Written into the PIMS |
| Front desk output | Recording | Message | Written summary plus booked slot |
| Clinical advice given | Not applicable | Varies | Never |

An answering service is not a bad product; it is a different product. It solves the ringing, not the decision. The comparison that matters is whether the call ends with an appointment in the schedule or a note to call back.

## Where it fits and where it does not

It fits an independent small-animal practice running one to six veterinarians, with a PIMS and no overnight front desk. It does not fit a practice that wants a clinical opinion delivered by phone at 3am, or one whose after-hours policy is to send every call to a dedicated emergency hospital regardless of presentation. In that second case the correct behaviour is to route, and the system can be configured to route.

## Choosing between this and a traditional answering service

The question to put to any provider is what happens between the caller's first sentence and the appointment appearing on the schedule. If the answer involves a human at the practice calling back, the after-hours problem is still there in the morning. If the answer involves a decision made against a script the provider wrote, the practice has handed its triage policy to a third party.
