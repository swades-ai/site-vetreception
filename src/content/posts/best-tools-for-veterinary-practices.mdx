The best software for veterinary practices is not one product. It is four layers that have to work together: the PIMS that holds the record, the phone layer that answers, the triage layer that decides urgency, and the scheduling layer that fills the book. Most practices running one to six veterinarians already own two of them and are missing the third.

This page compares those layers, says which ones decide most purchases, and shows where a phone layer that writes back into the PIMS changes the arithmetic.

## The four layers, and which one is usually broken

### The PIMS: the record of truth

Avimark, Cornerstone, ezyVet, Shepherd and Vetspire all hold the patient record, the client record and the appointment book. Nothing you add should create a second calendar. If a tool books appointments somewhere other than the PIMS, your front desk reconciles two systems by hand every morning, which is the cost you were trying to remove.

See how booking lands in each system: [Avimark integration](/blog/avimark-integration/), [Cornerstone practice management phone](/blog/cornerstone-practice-management-phone/), [ezyVet integration](/blog/ezyvet-integration/), [Shepherd veterinary software integration](/blog/shepherd-veterinary-software-integration/), [Vetspire integration](/blog/vetspire-integration/).

### The phone layer: who answers at 3am

An independent practice with no overnight front desk has three options for the phone: let it ring to voicemail, send it to a human answering service, or put an automated layer in front that answers, triages and books. Voicemail loses the call. A human service answers but usually leaves a message for the morning. The third option changes what your front desk walks into.

VetReception answers every call a veterinary practice cannot get to — after hours, during surgery, and at three in the morning — triages it against the practice's own written emergency protocol rather than a generic script, books the appointment straight into the PIMS, and leaves the front desk a written summary instead of a voicemail to work through.

### The triage layer: generic urgency versus your protocol

This is the layer buyers underweight. A generic urgency model asks whether a call is an emergency and sorts it into a bucket. Your practice already has a written protocol saying what a distended abdomen at 11pm means, who to call, and whether the client drives to you or to the emergency hospital across town. Triage that ignores your protocol produces bookings your vets did not agree to.

The distinction that matters: triage follows the practice's own written emergency protocol, not a generic urgency model. If a vendor cannot tell you how your protocol gets loaded and who signs off on changes, press on that before anything else.

### The scheduling layer: booking, not messages

A booking is written into the PIMS with the client, the patient and the reason for the visit attached. A message is a note someone reads later. Ask any vendor which one you get, and ask to see it happen on your own system rather than in a demo tenant.

## Where the money actually goes

PIMS licences are a fixed monthly cost per practice, sometimes per vet. Human answering services are commonly billed per call or per minute, so the cost rises in exactly the weeks you are busiest — the overnight emergency, the holiday weekend. Automated answering is typically priced per practice or by call-volume band, so a busy month costs the same as a quiet one.

## The questions that separate the options

Ask every vendor these four, in this order.

1. Does the booking land in my PIMS, and which ones do you support?
2. Whose protocol decides urgency — mine or yours?
3. What does my front desk receive in the morning?
4. What happens when a caller describes something clinical?

On the fourth: the AI never gives clinical advice. Anything clinical is routed to a named person at your practice. If a vendor's answer to that question is vague, that is your answer.

## How to run the comparison yourself

Pick one week. Count the calls that arrive outside staffed hours, count how many became appointments, and count how many your team called back the next morning. That ratio — calls answered against calls converted — is the number every vendor on this page competes to change. Run it before you take a demo, so the demo is measured against your own week rather than a vendor's.
