Veterinary client communication begins with whether the phone gets answered at all. A practice that answers every call, sorts it against the protocol its own veterinarians wrote, and books the appointment into the PIMS has solved the part of client communication that happens before the client ever reaches the building.

The rest of this page is about the mechanics of that: what the caller hears, what the front desk receives, what the system refuses to do, and how it compares to the options a practice is already weighing.

## Why the phone is the weak link in client communication

Client communication is usually discussed as an exam-room skill — explaining a treatment plan, handling cost conversations, following up after surgery. Those matter. But the first exchange a practice has with a client is almost always a phone call, and it is the one most likely to fail, because it arrives at a time when nobody is free to take it.

A practice running one to six veterinarians has a front desk that is also the pharmacy counter, the check-in desk, and the person holding a dog for a radiograph. The phone rings during all of it. After the last appointment, during a surgery block, and between midnight and the first morning arrival, there is often nobody at the desk at all.

### What a missed call actually costs

The cost is not the call. It is the appointment that never got booked, the client who dialled the next practice on the list, and the staff time the following morning spent listening to a recording and re-entering it into the PIMS by hand.

A missed call is also invisible in most practice reporting. The phone system logs it; the PIMS does not, because nothing was created. That is why missed-call volume tends to be underestimated by the people closest to it — the front desk remembers the calls it handled, not the ones that rang out while it was in the treatment room.

## What a call has to produce to count as handled

A call is only handled if it produces three things: an answer, a routing decision, and a record. Most setups deliver one or two.

A voicemail produces a recording but no routing decision and no answer in the moment. A generic answering service produces an answer and a message, but the routing decision is made against a script that does not know your patients or your history fields. A staff member on call produces all three, until the second call of the night arrives while they are driving in.

The distinction that matters is whether the routing decision is made against something the practice controls. A generic urgency model asks how urgent this is and returns a number. A protocol-driven system asks the questions your practice already agreed on: how long has the animal been straining, is there a known cardiac history, has the pet been hit by a car, can the owner come in tonight.

## Triage against the practice's own written emergency protocol

The boundary cases are where generic models fail. Your protocol might treat a vomiting dog with a known foreign-body surgery history as a same-night call and a vomiting dog with no history as a next-morning call. A model that does not know your history fields cannot make that distinction, so it either over-routes — sending everything in — or under-routes and misses the one that mattered.

VetReception triages against the practice's own written emergency protocol rather than a generic script. The questions asked on the call are the questions your veterinarians already wrote down, and the routing decision follows the same logic your team would use at the desk. When the protocol changes, the questions change with it.

### What the caller hears, in order

The caller hears a greeting in the practice's name, then a short set of questions drawn from the protocol. If the answers cross a threshold, the caller is told what to do next — come in now, call a named number, or expect a call back within a stated window. If the answers do not cross a threshold, the caller is offered an appointment slot from the practice's live schedule.

At no point does the system tell the caller what is wrong with the animal. It never gives clinical advice. Anything clinical is routed to a named person at the practice, with the caller's answers attached, and the practice chooses who that person is before go-live.

### What the front desk gets in the morning

The front desk does not get a voicemail. It gets a written summary: caller name, pet name, the protocol questions asked, the answers given, the routing decision, and the appointment that was booked. The summary is attached to the client record, so the person checking the pet in at 8am already knows why the call happened and does not have to reconstruct it from a recording.

That is the difference between a queue of tasks and a queue of decisions already made. A voicemail has to be listened to, interpreted, and re-entered into the PIMS by someone who was not on the call and cannot ask a follow-up question. A summary attached to an appointment has already been through all three steps.

## Booking written into the PIMS, not into a message

A booking that lives in a message is not a booking. It is a promise to make a booking later, and at 8am it competes with every other task the front desk has.

VetReception writes the appointment straight into the PIMS. The slot is held, the client record is updated, and the appointment appears in the schedule the same way a front-desk booking does. The practice's software stays the system of record; the phone system is a writer into it, not a parallel calendar that has to be reconciled.

Two consequences follow. Double-booking disappears, because the phone system and the front desk read the same schedule. And the call summary sits with the appointment, so the veterinarian opening the record sees the triage answers without asking anyone.

## The calls that arrive when nobody is at the desk

A practice with no overnight front desk has three windows where the phone is effectively unattended: after the last appointment, during surgery blocks, and between midnight and the first morning arrival.

VetReception answers every call a veterinary practice cannot get to — after hours, during surgery, and at three in the morning. That includes the ones that arrive at 3am, which are also the calls most likely to be a genuine emergency and most likely to be lost to whichever practice answers next.

During surgery the front desk is often physically in the treatment room, and the phone still rings. The difference between a system that answers and one that does not is whether the practice captures a same-day appointment or explains a missed call the next morning.

### Where refill and routine calls fit

Not every call is an emergency. A large share of incoming volume is refill requests, appointment changes, and questions about hours or directions. These do not need a veterinarian, but they do need to be logged and routed, and they should not compete with emergency calls for the same attention.

A [prescription refill request line](/blog/prescription-refill-request-line/) handles the refill category separately, so the refill workflow does not interrupt triage. The same logic applies to scheduling: [veterinary appointment scheduling software](/blog/veterinary-appointment-scheduling-software/) is only useful if the booking lands in the PIMS rather than a separate calendar.

## What the system will not do

The boundaries are worth stating plainly, because they are the reason it is safe to put in front of a client.

- It does not give clinical advice. Any question requiring a clinical answer is routed to a named person.
- It does not replace the front desk. It removes the calls the front desk physically cannot take and returns them as written summaries.
- It does not invent a protocol. The triage logic is built from the practice's own written emergency protocol, which the practice approves before go-live.
- It does not tell the caller what is wrong with the animal. It asks the questions the practice wrote and routes on the answers.

## What setup involves

Setup is built around the protocol, not the phone number. The practice supplies its written emergency protocol — the same document the veterinarians already use — and the triage questions are derived from it. The practice reviews and approves the routing rules before any call is handled.

The PIMS connection is configured next, so the system can read the live schedule and write appointments back. Most practices run a period where calls are handled and summaries are reviewed before the system is left to book unattended.

Nothing about the front desk's workflow changes except the input. Instead of a voicemail queue there is a summary queue, and each summary is already attached to an appointment.

## What to check before you commit

Four questions determine whether a system will work in a given practice.

1. Does it read and write your specific PIMS, or only export a summary?
2. Can the triage questions be edited when your protocol changes?
3. What happens to a call that crosses a threshold — who is named as the contact, and how are they reached?
4. What does the front desk actually see the next morning, and where does it live?

If the answer to the first question is that you receive an email, the system is a message service with better branding. The point of [written into the PIMS](/works-with-the-software-veterinary-practices-already-run/) is that the booking is real the moment the call ends.
